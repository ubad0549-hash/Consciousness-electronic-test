# The epistemology rests on many assumptions, and many claims remain unclarified. But I believe it is a necessary(a base of reference to make things coherent) condition for us to make claims about how we define consciousness and the categorizations we use to define it, and what counts as coherent versus incorrect.

First, let's look at the conversation regarding whether there is such a thing as pre-existing (a priori) knowledge. There are three answers to this: those who say it exists, those who say it doesn't, and those who fall somewhere in between.

First, I would like to start with the argument that pre-existing knowledge does not exist, or at least that its existence is very unlikely. The argument states that all knowledge must be intelligible, and through experience, we come to understand things. Through experience, we create concepts that depend on our experiences to be determined and understood. Because every piece of information we learn—or any concept we define or internalize—must come through our experience, claiming there is such a thing above experience that allows for understanding is a hypothesis derived from experience itself. Any claim that something is understandable separate from experience is determined by experience itself; therefore, there is no such thing as knowledge not determined by experience. So, the claim of pre-existing knowledge is more of a hypothesis on how we acquire knowledge, rather than a factual statement on how knowledge is formed.

The argument for the existence of pre-existing knowledge is that there are truths that are structurally true in and of themselves. That structure has a continuity that allows it to be defined and interpreted by us. In this view, pre-existing knowledge is the very structure that allows us to determine other structures. There are certain patterns and facts that are true in themselves. If we define an object, it has a continuous structure. While we might determine these structures using our own interpersonal language, the structures themselves are already pre-existing. Our description is merely an abstraction of the form, which in itself is pre-existing knowledge.

The third perspective is a mix of both: experience is needed to determine what counts as structure or what is intelligible, but the structure itself is a fact of experience, not separate from it. By experiencing, we allow ourselves to engage in an experience that contains these components within itself. There is no difference between the determination of the structure and the definition of the structure; both are conceptual. They both exist in the domain of experience. We do not necessarily find knowledge outside of experience, but rather determine knowledge within it; the pre-existing knowledge itself is a component by which we experience the world. The foundation of the definitions we reach through abstraction is grounded in experience itself. Experience has compositions that allow us to express it in form, and those compositions themselves are the pre-existing knowledge.

I personally lean toward the last point of view: the determinations of concepts are recognized through our experience, and those base components and structures within experience are the preconditions that allow us to describe that experience or other concepts.

That is my model of justification. When you justify something, you must consolidate a network into a discrete point that is included in the system. But where do the original discrete points in the system come from? Where do the first relations come from? And is it from those base components that we make justifications to create more elaborate conceptual abstractions?

I would assume there exist those pre-existing conditions that include discrete points, and from those points arises contextual meaning. From this process, we can elaborate on more complex justifications and understandings. We come to understand our structure through experiencing and gaining concepts to describe these pre-existing structures. These structures might be functional in subsets, but can they truly be justified? And that question must be determined by rationality. 

I would say rationality confirms itself because it seems to be the very process by which we come to understand. By making our justification process more rigorous and functionally driven—something we can testit grounds us, keeping us from relying entirely on guesswork and pure abstraction. Thus, we come to know ourselves by our nature, which is to justify and understand. We come to know ourselves more fully; the knowledge that was once just a possibility within us is now being understood through us. 

# This is dense and might be confusing to people who do not have the conceptual background.
My main claim is that understanding and categorization depend on our ability to define entities in an intelligible manner. Understanding can only come through experiencing both what is understood and what is not. The contrast between these states allows us to determine the identities of certain entities; for example, we know white is white because it is not black—this contrast defines its identity. This assumes that we must first establish foundational identities in order to describe and assign identities to other entities (where an "identity," in this case, can simply be a description). From these pre-existing conditions, we build context-dependent meaning. This creates new justifications for our descriptions of identity, broadening our overall structure of understanding. We can infer that the models we create from these justifications and abstractions are the ones that prove most useful to us. In this context, functionality refers to a framework that is coherent, reliable, testable, and rigorous, making it highly dependable. Rationality, then, is simply the process by which we build and refine this framework.
